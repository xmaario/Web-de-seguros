Do You Need Flood Insurance? A Homeowner's Guide

Published November 3, 2026 · 3 min read

Many homeowners assume their regular policy covers flooding. In most cases it does not. Flood insurance is a separate purchase, and deciding whether you need it starts with understanding your risk and how the coverage works.

Key takeaways

  • Standard homeowners and renters policies usually exclude flood damage.
  • Floods can happen outside high-risk zones.
  • NFIP policies generally have a 30-day waiting period, with some exceptions.
  • Compare NFIP and private flood options before you need them.

Why your homeowners policy probably does not cover floods

Standard homeowners and renters policies generally exclude damage from flooding, which includes overflowing rivers, storm surge, heavy rain that causes water to rise and enter your home from outside, and mudflow. This is different from sudden water damage inside the home, such as a burst pipe, which a homeowners policy may cover. Check your policy's exclusions to see exactly how flood is defined.

Who may need flood insurance?

If your lender requires it

If your home is in a high-risk flood area, known as a Special Flood Hazard Area, and you have a mortgage from a federally regulated or insured lender, you will generally be required to carry flood insurance. Your lender will tell you if this applies.

If you live outside a high-risk zone

Floods can happen almost anywhere. Heavy rain, snowmelt, overwhelmed drainage systems, and new development that changes how water flows can all cause flooding in areas not mapped as high risk. A significant share of flood insurance claims come from properties outside high-risk zones. If flooding is possible where you live, it is worth looking at your options even if no one requires you to buy coverage.

How to check your flood risk

  1. Look up your flood zone. FEMA publishes flood maps you can search by address through its Flood Map Service Center.
  2. Review local history. Ask neighbors, your local government, or the property's previous owners whether the area has flooded before.
  3. Check your property's features. Low-lying land, basements, and proximity to rivers, lakes, coastlines, or drainage channels can increase risk.
  4. Consider disclosure records. Some states require sellers to disclose past flood damage. Review any disclosures you received when you bought your home.

Your main options

The National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP)

The NFIP is a federal program managed by FEMA. Policies are sold through many insurance companies and agents, but coverage terms are set by the program. Key points include:

  • For a single-family home, building coverage is limited to $250,000 and contents coverage to $100,000.
  • Building and contents coverage are purchased separately. Contents coverage is not automatic.
  • There is generally a 30-day waiting period before a new policy takes effect, with some exceptions, such as when coverage is required in connection with a new mortgage.
  • Coverage for basements and areas below the lowest elevated floor is limited.
  • Personal property is generally covered at actual cash value rather than replacement cost.

Your community must participate in the NFIP for you to buy a policy through the program. Most communities do.

Private flood insurance

Some private insurers offer flood coverage, which may provide higher limits, replacement cost coverage, additional living expenses, or shorter waiting periods. Availability, terms, and prices vary by state and provider. If your lender requires flood insurance, confirm that a private policy meets their requirements before you buy.

What flood insurance typically covers

  • Building coverage: the structure and its foundation, electrical and plumbing systems, HVAC equipment, water heaters, built-in appliances, permanently installed carpeting, and detached garages in some cases.
  • Contents coverage: personal belongings such as clothing, furniture, and electronics, as well as certain appliances like portable air conditioners, washers, and dryers.

Common limitations include damage from moisture or mold that could have been avoided, cash and valuable papers, vehicles, and most property outside the building, such as landscaping, decks, and pools. Read the policy for the full list.

Do not wait for the forecast

Because NFIP policies usually have a 30-day waiting period, you generally cannot buy coverage when a storm is on the way and expect it to apply. If you are considering flood insurance, it is best to decide well before the season when storms or snowmelt are common in your area.

What about renters?

Renters can buy contents-only flood coverage to protect their belongings. The landlord's building policy, if any, does not cover a tenant's possessions. Learn more in our renters insurance basics guide.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • What is my flood zone, and has the area flooded before?
  • Do I need both building and contents coverage?
  • What are the deductibles for building and contents?
  • Is personal property covered at replacement cost or actual cash value?
  • When would coverage begin?
  • Does a private policy meet my lender's requirements?

The bottom line

Most homeowners policies do not cover flood damage, and floods can happen outside high-risk zones. Check your flood map and local history, compare NFIP and private options, and buy coverage well before you need it. A licensed agent can help you understand which option fits your home.
