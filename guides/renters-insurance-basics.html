Renters Insurance Basics: What It Covers and How Much You Need

By the [BRAND NAME] editorial team · Updated November 3, 2026 · 4 min read

If you rent, your landlord's insurance protects the building, not your belongings or your personal liability. Renters insurance fills that gap, and it is often less expensive than people expect. Here is how it works and how to choose coverage.

What renters insurance covers

A typical renters policy, sometimes called an HO-4 policy, includes three main types of coverage.

Personal property

Helps pay to repair or replace your belongings, such as furniture, clothing, electronics, and kitchenware, after a covered event like fire, smoke, theft, vandalism, or certain types of water damage. Coverage often extends to belongings temporarily away from home, such as a laptop stolen from your car or luggage stolen while traveling, subject to policy terms.

Personal liability

Helps protect you if you are legally responsible for injuring someone or damaging their property. For example, if a guest is hurt in your apartment, or water from your overflowing bathtub damages the unit below, liability coverage may help pay for damages and legal defense costs up to your limit.

Loss of use

If a covered event makes your rental unlivable, this coverage can help pay for temporary housing, meals, and other additional living expenses while repairs are made.

Many policies also include a small amount of medical payments coverage for guests who are injured in your home, regardless of fault.

What renters insurance usually does not cover

  • The building itself: structural damage is the landlord's responsibility and is covered by their policy.
  • Flood and earthquake: these are generally excluded and require separate coverage. See Do You Need Flood Insurance?
  • Roommates' belongings: each roommate usually needs their own policy unless they are specifically named.
  • Pest damage and wear and tear.
  • High-value items above sub-limits: jewelry, watches, collectibles, and similar items may have limited theft coverage unless you schedule them separately.
  • Business property: equipment used for a business may have limited coverage.

Exclusions vary by state and provider, so review the policy before you buy.

Why your landlord may require it

Many landlords require tenants to carry renters insurance, often with a minimum liability limit. This helps reduce disputes when a tenant accidentally causes damage or injury. If your lease requires coverage, you may need to list your landlord as an "interested party" so they are notified if your policy is canceled.

How much personal property coverage do you need?

The easiest way to estimate is to make a quick home inventory. Walk through each room and estimate what it would cost to replace your belongings today. People often underestimate the total, especially for clothing, kitchen items, and electronics. Taking photos or a video of each room also creates a useful record if you ever need to file a claim.

Once you have a rough total, choose a personal property limit that would cover it. If you own valuable items such as engagement rings or musical instruments, ask about scheduling them for additional coverage.

Replacement cost or actual cash value?

This choice can make a big difference after a loss. Actual cash value coverage subtracts depreciation, so a five-year-old television may be worth only a fraction of the cost of a new one. Replacement cost coverage pays to replace items with new ones of similar kind and quality, usually for a somewhat higher premium. Read our guide to replacement cost vs. actual cash value for more detail.

How much liability coverage do you need?

Renters policies commonly offer liability limits starting around $100,000, with higher options available. If you have savings or other assets to protect, or if you often host guests or have a dog, a higher limit may be worth considering. Your lease may also set a minimum. Note that some policies limit or exclude liability for certain dog breeds or other animals, so ask if this applies to you.

Choosing a deductible

The deductible is the amount you pay toward a personal property claim before coverage applies. A higher deductible usually lowers your premium. Choose one you could pay without difficulty, and remember that small losses may not be worth claiming at all.

What affects the price?

Renters insurance prices vary by state and provider. Common factors include your location, the amount of coverage and deductible you choose, whether you choose replacement cost coverage, your claims history, and in some states your credit-based insurance score. Safety features such as smoke detectors, sprinklers, and secured entries may qualify for discounts with some providers, and many insurers offer a discount if you bundle renters and auto insurance.

Steps to get covered

  1. Check your lease for any insurance requirements.
  2. Make a home inventory and estimate the total value of your belongings.
  3. Decide on replacement cost or actual cash value coverage.
  4. Choose a liability limit and a deductible.

  6. Add your landlord as an interested party if required.

The bottom line

Renters insurance can protect your belongings, your finances, and your housing if something goes wrong. Take an inventory, choose limits that reflect what you own and what you could lose in a lawsuit, and compare identical coverage from more than one provider. Review your policy when you move or make major purchases.
