Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?

Published November 4, 2026 · 4 min read

Water damage is one of the most common home insurance claims, and also one of the most misunderstood. Whether you are covered depends less on how much damage there is and more on where the water came from and how it happened.

Key takeaways

  • Sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst pipe, is often covered.
  • Floods, sewer backups, and slow leaks are usually excluded.
  • Water backup endorsements and flood insurance fill common gaps.
  • Stop the water, document the damage, and report it quickly.

The key question: sudden or gradual?

Standard homeowners policies generally cover water damage that is sudden and accidental, such as a pipe that bursts without warning. They generally do not cover damage that develops slowly over time, such as a small leak under a sink that went unnoticed or unrepaired for months. Insurers treat gradual damage as a maintenance issue.

Water damage that is often covered

  • Burst or frozen pipes, provided you took reasonable care, such as keeping the heat on. Some policies require you to maintain heat or shut off and drain the water supply if the home is vacant.
  • Accidental overflow from a washing machine, dishwasher, water heater, or bathtub.
  • Water from firefighting after a fire.
  • Roof leaks caused by a covered event, such as a storm tearing off shingles. See Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Roof Damage?
  • Ice dams in many policies, when melting snow backs up under shingles.

When a covered pipe or appliance fails, many policies pay to tear out and replace the parts of the building needed to reach the problem, such as a wall or floor, and to repair the resulting damage. They often do not pay to repair the failed pipe or appliance itself.

Water damage that is usually not covered

  • Flooding from rising water, heavy rain that enters from outside, storm surge, or overflowing rivers. This requires separate flood insurance. Read Do You Need Flood Insurance?
  • Sewer or drain backup, unless you add a water backup endorsement.
  • Gradual leaks and seepage over weeks or months.
  • Groundwater seeping through foundations or basement walls.
  • Mold, rot, and deterioration caused by long-term moisture. Some policies provide limited mold coverage when it results from a covered loss.
  • Neglect, such as ignoring a known leak.

Endorsements that can fill the gaps

  • Water backup coverage: for water that backs up through sewers or drains, or from a failed sump pump.
  • Service line coverage: for buried water and sewer lines on your property.
  • Higher mold limits, where available.
  • Separate flood insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer.

Availability, limits, and costs vary by state and provider. These options are often inexpensive relative to the cost of a single uncovered loss, so they are worth asking about.

What to do when water damage happens

  1. Stop the water. Know where your main water shut-off valve is before you need it.
  2. Stay safe. Turn off electricity in affected areas if it is safe to do so.
  3. Document the damage with photos and video before cleanup.
  4. Prevent further damage. Remove standing water and start drying out the area. Policies typically require reasonable steps to limit damage, and waiting can lead to mold.
  5. Keep receipts for emergency repairs, fans, and water removal services.
  6. Contact your insurer promptly and follow the steps in our deductibles guide to understand what you will pay.

How to prevent water damage

  • Inspect hoses on washing machines, dishwashers, and refrigerators, and replace worn ones.
  • Insulate pipes in unheated areas and keep the heat on during cold weather.
  • Keep gutters and downspouts clear so water drains away from the foundation.
  • Test your sump pump and consider a battery backup.
  • Consider water leak detectors or an automatic shut-off valve. Some insurers offer discounts for them.
  • Fix small leaks promptly. They rarely stay small.

Renters and condo owners

Renters insurance can cover your belongings damaged by a covered water event, but not the building itself. Condo owners should check how their association's master policy and their own HO-6 policy share responsibility. See Renters Insurance Basics and Condo Insurance (HO-6) Explained.

Common questions

Is a leaking water heater covered?

If the tank bursts suddenly, the resulting water damage is often covered, but the water heater itself usually is not. If it leaked slowly over time, the damage may be excluded as gradual.

Does homeowners insurance cover a leaking roof?

It depends on why the roof leaks. A leak caused by storm damage is often covered; a leak from an old, worn roof usually is not.

Will a water damage claim raise my premium?

It may. Some insurers consider prior water claims when pricing or renewing a policy, because they can signal future risk. Practices vary by state and provider.

The bottom line

Homeowners insurance generally covers sudden, accidental water damage but not floods, sewer backups without an endorsement, or slow leaks. Know your policy's exclusions, consider water backup and flood coverage, and maintain your home to prevent problems. Coverage varies by state and provider, so ask your insurer about specific situations.
