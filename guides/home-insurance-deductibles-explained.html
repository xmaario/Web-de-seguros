Home Insurance Deductibles Explained

By the PlainCover editorial team · Updated November 3, 2026 · 4 min read

Your deductible is one of the few parts of a homeowners policy you can easily adjust, and it shapes both your premium and what you pay out of pocket after a loss. Here is how deductibles work and how to choose one with confidence.

What a deductible is

A deductible is the portion of a covered loss that you pay yourself before your insurance pays the rest. If your home suffers a covered loss of $12,000 and your deductible is $1,000, the insurer would typically pay $11,000, subject to your policy limits and terms. If a loss costs less than your deductible, you would usually pay the whole amount and there would be no insurance payment.

Unlike health insurance, homeowners deductibles usually apply each time you file a claim rather than once per year. Two separate claims in the same year may mean paying two deductibles. Check your policy for the exact rules, because they vary by state and provider.

Flat-dollar deductibles

The most familiar type is a flat-dollar deductible, such as $500, $1,000, or $2,500. It stays the same no matter how large the claim is. Flat-dollar deductibles are easy to understand and budget for, which is why many homeowners prefer them for everyday risks like fire, theft, or a burst pipe.

Percentage deductibles

Some policies use a deductible that is a percentage of your dwelling coverage limit, not of the claim amount. This is an important difference. If your home is insured for $350,000 and your deductible is 2 percent, you would pay the first $7,000 of a covered loss. A percentage deductible grows as your coverage limit grows, so it can be much larger than people expect.

Percentage deductibles may apply to the whole policy or only to certain types of damage. Always confirm the dollar amount so you know exactly what you would owe.

Wind, hail, and hurricane deductibles

In areas exposed to severe weather, policies often include a separate deductible for specific perils. Common examples include:

  • Hurricane or named-storm deductibles, which may apply only when damage is caused by a storm that a weather agency has named or classified as a hurricane. The exact trigger is defined in your policy and, in some states, by state law.
  • Wind and hail deductibles, which may apply to any windstorm or hailstorm, not just named storms.
  • Earthquake deductibles, which apply to separate earthquake policies or endorsements and are often a high percentage of the coverage limit.

These special deductibles are usually percentage-based and can be several times larger than your regular deductible. A homeowner might have a $1,000 deductible for most claims and a 5 percent deductible for hurricane damage, which on a $400,000 dwelling limit would mean $20,000 out of pocket before coverage applies.

How your deductible affects your premium

In general, a higher deductible means a lower premium, because you agree to absorb more of each loss yourself. A lower deductible means a higher premium. How much the premium changes depends on the insurer, your location, and your home, so the only way to know is to ask for quotes at several deductible levels.

When you compare, look at the difference in premium alongside the difference in deductible. If raising your deductible by $1,000 lowers your annual premium only slightly, it may take many claim-free years to come out ahead. If the savings are larger, the trade-off may be more attractive. Either way, the decision depends on your own finances.

How to choose the right deductible

  1. Check your emergency fund. Choose a deductible you could pay quickly without borrowing or falling behind on other bills.
  2. Consider your claim habits. Many homeowners use insurance only for large losses and pay small repairs themselves. If that fits you, a higher deductible may make sense.
  3. Calculate percentage deductibles in dollars. Multiply the percentage by your dwelling limit and ask whether you could cover that amount after a major storm.
  4. Ask your lender. Some mortgage lenders set a maximum deductible you are allowed to carry.
  5. Revisit after changes. If you increase your dwelling coverage, a percentage deductible will rise too.

Deductibles and small claims

Even when a loss is slightly larger than your deductible, filing a claim is a personal choice. Some insurers may consider your claims history when setting future premiums, and practices vary by state and provider. If you are unsure whether to file, you can ask your insurer or agent how a claim might be handled, though you should be aware that some providers may record the inquiry. Weigh the expected payment against any potential long-term effect.

Common questions

Does the deductible apply to liability claims?

Usually not. Deductibles generally apply to damage to your own home and belongings, not to liability claims made by others against you. Check your policy for specifics.

Can I have different deductibles for different coverages?

Often, yes. Many policies have a standard deductible for most perils and separate deductibles for wind, hail, hurricanes, or earthquakes. Some providers also let you choose different amounts for dwelling and personal property.

Will the insurer subtract the deductible from my payment?

Typically, the deductible is subtracted from the claim payment rather than paid to the insurer separately. If you have a mortgage, the payment may be issued jointly to you and your lender.

The bottom line

Your deductible is a trade-off between what you pay every year and what you pay when something goes wrong. Know every deductible on your policy in dollar terms, especially any percentage deductibles for storms. Then pick amounts you could genuinely afford.
