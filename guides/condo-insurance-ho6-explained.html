Condo Insurance (HO-6) Explained

Published November 4, 2026 · 4 min read

Owning a condo means sharing responsibility for the building with your neighbors and your condo association. Your own policy, usually called an HO-6 policy, fills in what the association's insurance does not cover. Knowing where one ends and the other begins is the key to choosing the right coverage.

Key takeaways

  • Your association's master policy and your HO-6 policy share responsibility.
  • The type of master policy determines how much of your interior you must insure.
  • Loss assessment coverage can help pay your share of certain association charges.
  • Read the master policy summary and association rules before choosing limits.

Two policies, one building

Most condo associations carry a master policy that covers the building's shared structure and common areas, such as the roof, exterior walls, hallways, and amenities, along with liability for those areas. Your HO-6 policy covers what belongs to you: your unit's interior to the extent the master policy does not, your belongings, your personal liability, and additional living expenses if your unit becomes unlivable.

Types of master policies

The amount of your unit the master policy covers depends on its type. Terms vary, but you will often see three broad approaches:

  • Bare walls: the master policy covers the structure but not interior items like drywall, cabinets, flooring, fixtures, and appliances. You insure those.
  • Single entity or original specifications: the master policy covers interior fixtures as originally built, but not upgrades or improvements you or previous owners made.
  • All-in or all-inclusive: the master policy covers the interior, including many improvements. You still need coverage for your belongings and liability.

Ask your association for a copy of the master policy summary and read the association's governing documents, often called bylaws or CC&Rs, which spell out who is responsible for what.

What an HO-6 policy usually covers

  • Dwelling or building property: interior walls, floors, fixtures, cabinets, and improvements that the master policy does not cover.
  • Personal property: furniture, electronics, clothing, and other belongings.
  • Personal liability: if you are responsible for injuring someone or damaging their property, including damage to a neighbor's unit, such as from an overflowing bathtub.
  • Loss of use: temporary housing and extra costs if a covered loss forces you out.
  • Loss assessment: see below.

Loss assessment coverage

If the association's master policy does not fully pay for a loss, perhaps because of a large deductible or a claim that exceeds its limits, the association may charge each owner a share, known as an assessment. Loss assessment coverage can help pay your share of certain covered assessments. Many HO-6 policies include a small amount by default. Because master policy deductibles can be large, many owners choose to increase this limit. Ask how your policy handles assessments related to the master policy deductible.

How to choose your limits

  1. Dwelling coverage: estimate the cost to rebuild everything inside your unit that the master policy does not cover, including upgrades like new kitchens or flooring.
  2. Personal property: make a home inventory and estimate replacement costs. Consider replacement cost rather than actual cash value. See Replacement Cost vs. Actual Cash Value.
  3. Liability: choose a limit that reflects your assets. An umbrella policy can add more. See What Is Umbrella Insurance?
  4. Loss assessment: compare with the master policy's deductible and consider a higher limit.
  5. Deductible: choose an amount you could pay from savings.

Common gaps to watch for

  • Water damage from a neighbor's unit or shared pipes, where responsibility can be complicated. Read Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?
  • Sewer backup, which usually requires an endorsement.
  • Flood and earthquake, which are typically excluded from both the master policy and your own policy.
  • Valuable items such as jewelry, which may have low limits unless scheduled.
  • Renting out your unit, which may require different coverage.

Is condo insurance required?

No state law requires condo insurance, but many mortgage lenders require it, and many associations require owners to carry a policy with certain minimum limits. Check your loan documents and association rules.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • What type of master policy does the association have, and what is its deductible?
  • Who is responsible for pipes, appliances, and fixtures inside my unit?
  • Does the association require owners to carry minimum coverage?
  • How much loss assessment coverage is included, and can I increase it?
  • Is personal property covered at replacement cost?

Common questions

Does condo insurance cover my balcony or patio?

It depends on whether the balcony is part of your unit or a limited common element under the association's documents. Check the governing documents and ask both your insurer and the association.

Does HO-6 cover my neighbor's unit if my pipe leaks?

Your liability coverage may respond if you are legally responsible for the damage. The association's master policy may also be involved, depending on its terms.

The bottom line

An HO-6 policy covers what your association's master policy does not: your interior, your belongings, your liability, and your share of certain assessments. Start by reading the master policy summary and your association's rules, then choose limits that close the gaps. Coverage and prices vary by state and provider.
