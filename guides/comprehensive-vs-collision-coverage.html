Comprehensive vs. Collision Coverage: What's the Difference?

Published November 5, 2026 · Updated November 11, 2026 · 5 min read

Comprehensive and collision are the two coverages that pay to repair or replace your own car. They are often sold together and sometimes confused, but they cover different kinds of damage. Knowing which is which helps you read a quote, choose deductibles, and decide whether you need both.

Key takeaways

  • Collision covers damage from crashing into a vehicle or object, regardless of fault.
  • Comprehensive covers non-crash events such as theft, hail, fire, and animal strikes.
  • Lenders usually require both on financed or leased cars.
  • On an older, paid-off car, compare the yearly cost with what you could collect after a loss.

The short answer

Collision covers damage to your car when it hits, or is hit by, another vehicle or an object, regardless of who was at fault. Comprehensive covers damage from most other events that are not collisions, such as theft, fire, hail, or hitting an animal. Neither one pays for injuries or for damage you cause to other people's property; that is the job of liability coverage.

What collision coverage typically pays for

  • A crash with another vehicle, whether you or the other driver caused it.
  • Hitting a stationary object, such as a pole, fence, guardrail, or parked car.
  • A single-car accident, such as sliding off the road into a ditch.
  • A rollover.
  • Damage from potholes in many policies, when it results from striking the pothole.

If another driver caused the accident, you may be able to claim against their liability coverage instead. Using your own collision coverage can be faster, though you may pay your deductible first and then wait for your insurer to try to recover it from the other insurer.

What comprehensive coverage typically pays for

  • Theft of the vehicle, and in many policies damage caused during a break-in.
  • Vandalism.
  • Fire and explosions.
  • Weather events such as hail, windstorms, and flooding.
  • Falling objects, such as tree branches.
  • Hitting an animal, such as a deer.
  • Glass damage, such as a cracked windshield, in many policies.

Hitting an animal usually falls under comprehensive, while swerving to avoid an animal and hitting a tree is usually treated as a collision. The difference can matter because each coverage may have its own deductible.

What neither one covers

  • Normal wear and tear, mechanical breakdowns, and maintenance.
  • Injuries to you, your passengers, or other people.
  • Damage to other people's cars or property, which liability coverage handles.
  • Personal belongings stolen from the car, which are more often covered by homeowners or renters insurance.
  • Custom equipment above certain limits, unless you add coverage for it.

Comprehensive vs. collision deductible

Collision and comprehensive usually have separate deductibles, and you can often choose different amounts. Many drivers choose a lower comprehensive deductible because those claims, such as a cracked windshield or hail damage, can be frequent in some areas, and a higher collision deductible to keep the premium down. Read How Car Insurance Deductibles Work for more detail.

  • Which one applies: the collision deductible applies to crash claims; the comprehensive deductible applies to theft, weather, fire, glass, and animal claims.
  • Different amounts: for example, a policy might have a $1,000 collision deductible and a $500 comprehensive deductible. These figures are only an illustration.
  • Lender limits: if your car is financed or leased, the lender may set a maximum for each deductible.
  • Choosing amounts: pick deductibles you could pay quickly from savings, and compare the premium difference at each level.
Example

Hail dents your hood and a week later you back into a pole. The hail claim uses your comprehensive deductible; the pole claim uses your collision deductible. Figures and outcomes vary by policy.

How payouts work

Both coverages generally pay to repair your car, minus the deductible. If repairs would cost more than the car is worth, or close to it, the insurer may declare it a total loss and pay its actual cash value, minus the deductible. If you owe more on a loan or lease than the car is worth, gap insurance may cover the difference. See What Is Gap Insurance? For the step-by-step process, read how to file a car insurance claim.

Are they required?

No state requires collision or comprehensive by law. However, if you finance or lease a car, the lender or leasing company will almost always require both, often with a maximum deductible. Together with liability, they make up what many people call "full coverage." Read What Is Full Coverage Car Insurance?

How to decide whether you need them

  1. Check your loan or lease. If the car is financed or leased, you will likely need both.
  2. Estimate the car's value. Coverage pays no more than the car's actual cash value, minus the deductible.
  3. Compare the yearly cost. Ask for quotes with and without each coverage to see the real difference.
  4. Consider your savings. If you could not replace the car after a loss, keeping the coverage may be worth it.
  5. Think about local risks. High theft rates, frequent hail, or deer-heavy roads can make comprehensive more valuable.

Some owners of older cars keep comprehensive, which is often less expensive, and drop collision. Others raise both deductibles to lower the cost while keeping protection against a major loss. For more ways to save, see How to Lower Your Car Insurance Premium.

Common questions

Does comprehensive cover a cracked windshield?

Usually yes, subject to your comprehensive deductible. Some insurers offer full glass coverage with a lower or no deductible, and some waive the deductible for small chip repairs.

Can I buy comprehensive without collision?

Many insurers allow it, especially on older cars, but rules vary by provider. A lender, if you have one, will usually require both.

Should my comprehensive deductible be lower than my collision deductible?

Many drivers choose that setup because comprehensive claims, such as glass or hail damage, can be more frequent in some areas. It depends on your local risks, your savings, and the price difference.

Does collision cover hitting a deer?

Typically no. Hitting an animal is usually a comprehensive claim. Swerving and hitting something else instead is usually a collision claim.

The bottom line

Collision covers crashes; comprehensive covers most other damage to your car, such as theft, weather, fire, and animal strikes. Lenders usually require both. If you own your car outright, weigh the yearly cost against the car's value and your ability to replace it. Prices and terms vary by state and provider, so compare quotes with the same deductibles.
