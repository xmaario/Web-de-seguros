How Long Does an Accident Stay on Your Insurance Record?

Published by Mario González Ordóñez · Updated November 4, 2026 · 4 min read

After an accident, one of the first worries is how long it will affect your car insurance. The answer depends on several things: your state, your insurer's rules, whether you were at fault, and which record you mean. Here is how it generally works.

Key takeaways

  • Your driving record and your claims history are separate records.
  • Many insurers look back about three to five years when setting rates.
  • Claims reports can show up to seven years of history.
  • Check your records for errors and dispute mistakes.

Two different records

Insurers can look at more than one source of information about you, and each keeps information for different lengths of time.

Your driving record

Your state's motor vehicle agency keeps a driving record, sometimes called a motor vehicle report. It includes traffic violations, license suspensions, and, in many states, reportable accidents. How long items stay on it depends on state law and the type of violation. Minor items may drop off after a few years, while serious offenses such as driving under the influence can remain for much longer.

Your claims history

Insurers also share claims information through industry databases. One of the most widely used is the Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange, known as a CLUE report, maintained by a consumer reporting agency. These reports commonly cover up to seven years of auto and property claims. An insurer may see a claim on this report even if it does not appear on your driving record.

How long it affects your premium

An item being on a record is not the same as it affecting your price. Many insurers look back about three to five years when rating a policy for at-fault accidents and moving violations. After that period, an older accident may no longer raise your premium, even if it still appears in a database. The exact look-back period varies by insurer and state, and some states set limits on how insurers can use older incidents.

  • At-fault accidents commonly affect rates for several years.
  • Not-at-fault accidents generally have less effect, and some states limit or prohibit surcharges for them.
  • Minor tickets such as low-level speeding often affect rates for about three years.
  • Major violations such as DUI or reckless driving can affect rates for longer and may require an SR-22. See What Is an SR-22?
  • Comprehensive claims for events such as hail or theft are often treated differently from at-fault accidents, but practices vary.

Accident forgiveness

Some insurers offer accident forgiveness, which may prevent your first at-fault accident from raising your premium. It may be included automatically after a certain number of claim-free years or offered as a paid add-on. Terms differ: some programs apply only to one accident per policy period, and the benefit may not transfer if you switch insurers. Some states restrict these programs. Ask what is included before you rely on it.

How to check your records

  1. Driving record: request a copy from your state's motor vehicle agency. Many states offer online access for a small fee.
  2. CLUE report: under federal law, you are entitled to request a free copy of your consumer report from the agency that maintains it, generally once every 12 months. You can also request it if an insurer takes adverse action based on it.
  3. Review for errors: look for accidents assigned to you incorrectly, duplicate entries, or claims that were not yours.
  4. Dispute mistakes: consumer reporting agencies are required to investigate disputes about inaccurate information. Correcting an error may lower your premium.

What you can do in the meantime

  • Drive carefully. A new incident can extend the period of higher premiums.
  • Take a defensive driving course. In some states it can remove points or qualify you for a discount.
  • Raise your deductible if you have savings to cover it. See How Car Insurance Deductibles Work.
  • Compare quotes. Insurers weigh past accidents differently, so another company may offer a better price.
  • Ask about discounts you still qualify for. Our guide on lowering your premium lists common ones.

Should you report a minor accident?

Your policy likely requires you to report accidents promptly, especially if anyone was injured or another person's property was damaged. Failing to report could put your coverage at risk if a claim is made later. Reporting an accident is not always the same as filing a claim, but practices vary, so ask your insurer how it handles reports. Read How to File a Car Insurance Claim for the full process.

Common questions

Does a not-at-fault accident raise my premium?

It depends on your state and insurer. Some states restrict surcharges for accidents you did not cause, while some insurers may still consider overall claim frequency. Ask your insurer how it treats not-at-fault claims.

Will switching insurers erase an accident?

No. A new insurer can usually see your driving record and claims history, so the accident is likely to be considered there too.

The bottom line

An accident may stay in databases for years, but its effect on your premium often fades after three to five years if you keep a clean record. Check your driving record and claims report for errors, ask about accident forgiveness, and compare quotes once the incident ages. Rules vary by state and provider, so confirm the details with your insurer.
