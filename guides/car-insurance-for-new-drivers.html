Car Insurance for New Drivers: A Beginner's Guide

Published November 4, 2026 · 4 min read

Getting your first license is exciting, and insurance is one of the first practical decisions that comes with it. New drivers often face higher prices because they have no driving history. Understanding your options can help you get properly covered without overpaying.

Key takeaways

  • New drivers usually pay more because they have no driving history.
  • Joining a household policy is often cheaper than buying a separate one.
  • Good student discounts, driver training, and a sensible car can help.
  • A clean record in the first years shapes future premiums.

Why new drivers usually pay more

Insurers price policies based on the risk of a claim. Drivers with little experience, especially teenagers, are statistically more likely to be involved in accidents. Without a track record, an insurer has less information to judge how you drive. As you gain experience and keep a clean record, your premium may come down over time, although every insurer handles this differently.

Do you need insurance with a learner's permit?

Rules vary by state and insurer. In many cases, a driver with a learner's permit is covered under the policy of the household they live in while practicing with a licensed adult. Some insurers want permit holders listed on the policy; others do not require it until the driver is fully licensed. Call your family's insurer before practice driving begins and ask exactly what is required.

Joining a parent's policy or buying your own

Joining a household policy

Adding a new driver to an existing family policy is often less expensive than buying a separate policy. The household may keep multi-car, bundling, and loyalty discounts, and the new driver benefits from the policy's existing limits. Insurers generally expect all licensed drivers in a household to be disclosed, so adding a new driver is usually required anyway.

Buying your own policy

A separate policy may make sense if the new driver owns the car in their own name, lives away from home permanently, or if the family wants to keep risks separate. It usually costs more, but it builds an independent insurance history. Ask for quotes both ways so you can compare the real difference.

Coverage to think about

  • Liability: required in nearly every state. Because new drivers are more likely to be involved in accidents, many families choose limits well above the state minimum. Read How Much Car Insurance Do You Really Need?
  • Collision and comprehensive: important if the car is financed or would be hard to replace. For an older, inexpensive car, the cost may outweigh the benefit. See What Is Full Coverage Car Insurance?
  • Uninsured and underinsured motorist: protects the new driver if someone without enough insurance hits them.
  • Medical payments or personal injury protection: required in some states and useful in others.
  • Roadside assistance: can give peace of mind to a driver who has never dealt with a breakdown.

Ways to keep the cost manageable

  1. Ask about good student discounts. Many insurers offer one for students who maintain a certain grade average.
  2. Complete a driver education or defensive driving course. Some insurers offer a discount for approved courses, and the skills help too.
  3. Choose a sensible car. A modest, safe vehicle with good crash ratings and reasonable repair costs is usually cheaper to insure than a sports car or a large, powerful vehicle.
  4. Assign drivers carefully. In some policies, which driver is listed as the primary driver of which car can affect the price. Always list drivers accurately.
  5. Consider a usage-based program. Programs that reward safe driving can help new drivers prove themselves, but understand how the program can affect the rate.
  6. Look into a student away-at-school discount if a student lives far from home without a car.
  7. Compare quotes. The price of adding a young driver can vary widely between companies.

Building a good insurance record

Your first few years of driving shape your insurance costs for years afterward. Tickets and at-fault accidents can raise premiums for several years. Simple habits make a big difference: follow speed limits, never drive impaired, keep your phone out of reach, and limit the number of passengers in the car while you gain experience. Many states have graduated licensing rules that limit nighttime driving and passengers for new drivers, so make sure you know and follow them.

What to do after an accident

New drivers should know what to do before anything happens. Check for injuries, call for help if needed, exchange information, take photos, and contact the insurer promptly. Keep a copy of our claims checklist on your phone.

Common questions

Will my rate go down as I get older?

For many drivers, premiums tend to decrease with age and experience, provided the driving record stays clean. The amount and timing vary by insurer and state.

Can I drive a friend's car?

Coverage for borrowing a car depends on the car owner's policy and your own. Many policies extend coverage to occasional drivers with permission, but there are exceptions. Ask before you borrow.

Do I need insurance if I do not own a car?

If you drive regularly but do not own a car, a non-owner policy may provide liability coverage. Ask an insurer whether it fits your situation.

The bottom line

For most families, adding a new driver to an existing policy is the simplest and least expensive option, but it is worth comparing. Choose solid liability limits, ask about every discount, pick a sensible car, and focus on building a clean record. Prices vary by state and provider, so get quotes before the new driver hits the road. For a rough idea, try our estimate calculator.
