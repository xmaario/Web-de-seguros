How to read liability limits

Car liability limits are often written as three numbers, such as 100/300/100. In thousands of dollars, they mean:

  • Bodily injury per person: the most the policy pays for one injured person ($100,000 in the example).
  • Bodily injury per accident: the most it pays for all injuries in one accident ($300,000).
  • Property damage per accident: the most it pays for damage to other people's cars and property ($100,000).

Some policies use a single combined limit instead. Read How Much Car Insurance Do You Need? for a full walkthrough.

How the calculator works

A rule of thumb used in many consumer guides is to carry liability limits at least as high as the assets you would want to protect. The calculator adds up the assets you enter and picks the lowest common limit level that covers them. It suggests one level higher when you have more exposure, such as a teen driver, frequent passengers, or a higher income, since future wages may be at risk in a lawsuit in many states.

When your assets are higher than common car policy limits, it points you to umbrella insurance, which adds liability coverage on top of your car and home policies. Learn more in What Is Umbrella Insurance?

State minimums are a floor, not a target

Every state with required car insurance sets minimum liability limits, and many are low compared with the cost of a serious accident. Medical bills and vehicle repairs can exceed minimum limits quickly. Your state insurance department lists the current requirements where you live.

Common questions

Is 100/300/100 enough?

It is a level many consumer guides mention as a starting point, but whether it is enough depends on what you own, your income, and your risks. If your assets are higher, consider higher limits or an umbrella policy.

Does liability cover my own car?

No. Liability pays for other people's injuries and property. Damage to your own car falls under collision or comprehensive coverage.

Should my home policy liability match my car policy?

Many people choose similar limits on both, and umbrella insurers usually require minimum limits on each underlying policy.
