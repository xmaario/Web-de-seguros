What Is Full Coverage Car Insurance?

"Full coverage" is one of the most common phrases in car insurance, yet you will not find it as a defined product on most policies. It is shorthand for a combination of coverages, and what it includes can differ from one provider or lender to the next.

The short answer

When people say full coverage, they usually mean a policy that includes three things:

  • Liability coverage, which pays for injuries and property damage you cause to others. This is the part most states require.
  • Collision coverage, which helps pay to repair or replace your own car after a crash, regardless of fault.
  • Comprehensive coverage, which helps pay for damage from non-collision events such as theft, hail, fire, flooding, vandalism, or hitting an animal.

Depending on your state and provider, a "full coverage" quote may also include uninsured motorist coverage, medical payments, or personal injury protection. Because the phrase has no standard definition, always look at the list of coverages and limits on the quote rather than relying on the label.

Full coverage does not mean everything is covered

The name can be misleading. Even a policy with every major coverage has limits, deductibles, and exclusions. Some examples of things a typical policy may not cover, or may cover only with an add-on:

  • Normal wear and tear, mechanical breakdowns, and routine maintenance.
  • Personal belongings stolen from your car, which are more often handled under a homeowners or renters policy.
  • Custom parts and equipment above a certain value, unless you add coverage for them.
  • Using your personal car for ride-share or delivery work, which may require a special endorsement or a commercial policy.
  • The gap between your loan balance and the car's value if it is totaled, unless you have gap coverage.
  • A rental car while yours is repaired, unless you have rental reimbursement.

Liability also has dollar limits. If you cause a serious accident and the costs exceed your limits, you could be responsible for the difference. Full coverage with low liability limits may protect your car well while leaving your savings exposed.

When full coverage is required

No state requires collision or comprehensive by law. However, if you finance or lease a vehicle, your lender or leasing company will almost always require both, often with a maximum deductible. The lender has a financial interest in the car and wants it protected until the loan is paid off. If you let the coverage lapse, the lender may buy expensive coverage on your behalf and add the cost to your loan.

Check your loan or lease agreement for the exact requirements, including deductible limits and whether the lender must be listed on the policy as a loss payee.

How much more does it cost?

Adding collision and comprehensive increases your premium, sometimes significantly. The difference depends on your car's value and repair costs, where you live, your driving record, and the deductibles you choose, and it varies by state and provider. Newer and more expensive cars generally cost more to cover because they cost more to repair or replace.

To see the real difference for your situation, ask providers to quote the same liability limits with and without collision and comprehensive. Our estimate calculator can give you an illustrative starting range, but only a quote reflects your actual price.

When it may make sense to drop collision and comprehensive

Once your car is paid off, keeping full coverage becomes your decision. Consider these questions:

  1. What is the car worth today? Look up its approximate market value. Insurance generally pays no more than the car's actual cash value, minus your deductible.
  2. What does the coverage cost each year? Compare that to the most you could receive in a claim after the deductible.
  3. Could you replace the car yourself? If losing the car would leave you without transportation and without the money to replace it, the coverage may still be worth it.
  4. Where do you park and drive? High theft rates, frequent hail, or heavy traffic may make comprehensive or collision more valuable.

Some drivers keep comprehensive, which is often less expensive, and drop only collision. Others raise their deductibles to lower the cost while keeping protection against a major loss.

How to read a full coverage quote

When you compare quotes, write down each of these items so you can compare them line by line:

  • Bodily injury and property damage liability limits.
  • Uninsured and underinsured motorist limits.
  • Medical payments or personal injury protection, if included.
  • Collision deductible and comprehensive deductible.
  • Any add-ons, such as rental reimbursement, roadside assistance, or gap coverage.
  • The policy term, either six or twelve months, and any fees.

The bottom line

Full coverage usually means liability plus collision and comprehensive, but it is a label rather than a guarantee that every situation is covered. If you have a loan or lease, you will likely need it. If you own your car outright, weigh the yearly cost against the car's value and your ability to replace it. In every case, check the liability limits, because protecting your finances matters as much as protecting your vehicle.
