Liability Limits Explained: What 100/300/100 Means

Car insurance quotes often show liability coverage as three numbers separated by slashes, such as 25/50/25 or 100/300/100. Those numbers set the most your policy will pay if you cause an accident that injures other people or damages their property. Here is how to read them and how to think about choosing yours.

The short answer

In 100/300/100, the numbers are in thousands of dollars. Your policy may pay up to $100,000 for injuries to any one person, up to $300,000 for all injuries in one accident, and up to $100,000 for property damage you cause. If damages exceed those limits, you may be personally responsible for the rest.

The three numbers, one by one

  • First number, bodily injury per person: the most the policy pays for injuries to a single person in an accident you cause, such as their medical bills and lost wages.
  • Second number, bodily injury per accident: the most the policy pays for all injured people combined in one accident.
  • Third number, property damage per accident: the most the policy pays for damage to other people's property, such as their car, a fence, or a building.

Your insurer may also pay legal defense costs if you are sued over a covered accident. In many policies, those costs are paid in addition to your limits, but policy wording varies, so check yours.

Example

With 100/300/100 limits, suppose you cause a crash that injures three people. If one person's covered injury costs are $150,000, the policy would pay up to $100,000 for that person. All three people together could receive no more than $300,000. These figures are only an illustration.

Split limits vs. combined single limit

The three-number format is called split limits, because the coverage is split into separate caps. Some policies use a combined single limit instead: one amount, such as $300,000, that applies to all injuries and property damage from one accident together. A combined single limit can be more flexible, because the full amount can go wherever the damage is. Not every insurer offers it for personal auto policies.

What liability limits do not cover

  • Damage to your own car, which collision and comprehensive cover. See What Is Full Coverage Car Insurance?
  • Your own injuries or your passengers' injuries, which may be covered by medical payments, personal injury protection, or health insurance, depending on your state and policy.
  • Damage caused by a driver with too little insurance, which uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage is designed for.
  • Amounts above your limits.

Why state minimums may not be enough

Almost every state requires some liability coverage, and the minimums vary widely. Minimum limits are often low compared with the cost of a serious accident, because medical care and newer vehicles can be expensive. If a claim exceeds your limits, the injured person may be able to pursue you for the difference, which could put your savings or future income at risk. Raising limits often costs less than people expect, but the price difference varies by state and provider.

How to choose your limits

  1. Check your state minimum. Your state's department of insurance or motor vehicle agency lists it.
  2. Think about what you have to protect. Savings, home equity, and future income can all matter.
  3. Compare quotes at several levels. Ask for prices at two or three limit levels with the same deductibles.
  4. Consider an umbrella policy. If you need more protection, umbrella insurance can add coverage above your auto limits, and insurers usually require certain underlying limits first. See What Is Umbrella Insurance?
  5. Use a tool. Our liability coverage calculator can help you think through a range, and How Much Car Insurance Do You Need? explains the rest of your policy.

Common questions

Is 100/300/100 good coverage?

It is a level many people consider, and it is higher than the minimum in many states. Whether it is enough depends on what you have to protect and your budget.

Do higher limits raise my premium a lot?

Higher limits usually cost more, but the increase is often smaller than the increase in coverage. Compare real quotes to see the difference.

Does my liability coverage follow me in another car?

Often yes, when you drive a car you do not own with permission, though the car owner's policy may apply first. Rules vary by policy and state.

The bottom line

Liability limits like 100/300/100 set how much your policy pays for injuries and property damage you cause to others. State minimums are often low, and anything above your limits may come out of your pocket. Compare quotes at a few limit levels and choose the protection that fits your situation. Coverage and prices vary by state and provider.
