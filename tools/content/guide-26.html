What Happens When Your Car Is Totaled?

Hearing that your car is "totaled" can be stressful, especially if you still owe money on it or rely on it every day. Knowing how insurers make that decision, how the payout is figured, and what happens to your loan and title can help you understand the offer and avoid surprises.

The short answer

When repairs would cost about as much as, or more than, the car is worth, the insurer may declare it a total loss. If the damage is covered, it usually pays the car's actual cash value just before the loss, minus your deductible. If you have a loan, the lender is usually paid first, and the insurer typically takes the car.

How insurers decide a car is totaled

An adjuster estimates the cost to repair the car and compares it with the car's actual cash value. Many states set a total loss threshold, a percentage of the car's value at which the insurer must or may treat it as a total loss. Other states use a formula that compares repair costs plus the car's salvage value with its actual cash value. The rules vary by state and provider, so two similar cars may be treated differently in different places.

A car may also be considered a total loss if it is stolen and not recovered, or if damage such as flooding makes repairs unsafe or impractical.

How the payout is calculated

Actual cash value is generally what your car was worth just before the loss, not what you paid for it or what you owe. Insurers often estimate it using recent sales and listings of similar vehicles in your area, adjusted for:

  • Year, make, model, and trim.
  • Mileage.
  • Condition before the loss, including prior damage.
  • Options and equipment.

Your deductible is then subtracted. Some states require insurers to include sales tax, title, or registration fees in a total loss payment; ask your adjuster whether that applies to you. For how actual cash value differs from replacement cost, see Replacement Cost vs. Actual Cash Value.

Example

Suppose the insurer values your car at $15,000 and your collision deductible is $1,000. The settlement would be about $14,000, before any taxes or fees your state may require. These figures are only an illustration.

What happens with a car loan or lease

If the car is financed or leased, the insurer usually pays the lender or leasing company first. If the payment is more than the loan balance, the rest goes to you. If it is less, you may still owe the difference, even though you no longer have the car. Gap insurance may cover that shortfall if you have it. See What Is Gap Insurance? or take our gap insurance quiz to see whether it may apply to you.

Keep making loan payments until the lender confirms the loan is paid off, so a missed payment does not show up on your credit.

The usual steps after a total loss

  1. Get the valuation report. Ask how the insurer calculated the car's value and which comparable vehicles it used.
  2. Review it carefully. Check the trim, mileage, options, and condition listed.
  3. Remove your belongings and plates. Ask where the car is stored and whether your state requires you to return the plates.
  4. Sign the title and paperwork. The insurer usually takes ownership and sells the car for salvage.
  5. Check your rental coverage. Rental reimbursement often ends a set time after a settlement offer, so ask when yours ends.
  6. Cancel or update your policy. Once the claim is settled, update your coverage for your next car.

If the offer seems too low

  • Gather listings for similar cars for sale near you, with similar mileage and equipment.
  • Share maintenance records and receipts for recent major work, such as new tires.
  • Point out any errors in the valuation report, such as a lower trim level.
  • Ask whether your policy has an appraisal clause, which may let each side hire an appraiser to settle a disagreement over value.
  • If you cannot resolve it, you may contact your state's department of insurance.

Can you keep a totaled car?

In many states, you may be able to keep the car. The insurer typically subtracts its salvage value from your payment. The car may then need a salvage title, and it may have to pass an inspection before it can be driven again, often under a rebuilt or similar title. Rules vary by state, and some insurers may limit coverage on a rebuilt car.

Common questions

How long does a total loss claim take?

It often takes several weeks, especially when a lender and title paperwork are involved. See How Long Does a Car Insurance Claim Take?

Will a total loss raise my premium?

It may, especially if you were at fault. A not-at-fault loss may affect your premium less, depending on your state and insurer.

The bottom line

When your car is totaled, the insurer generally pays its actual cash value minus your deductible, and pays any lender first. Review the valuation, push back with evidence if it seems low, and check whether gap coverage applies if you owe more than the car is worth. Rules and payouts vary by state and provider.
