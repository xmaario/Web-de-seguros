Why Did My Car Insurance Go Up?

Opening a renewal notice and seeing a higher premium is frustrating, especially if you have not had an accident or a ticket. Rate changes can come from your own situation, from changes in your area, or from broader costs in the insurance market. Understanding the cause can help you decide what to do next.

Reasons related to you

A recent accident or claim

An at-fault accident is one of the most common reasons for an increase. Depending on your insurer and state, other claims may also be considered. The effect usually lasts for a set number of years, after which it may no longer apply. An at-fault accident or ticket can affect your price for years; see how long an accident stays on your insurance. Adding a teen to the policy is another common cause, covered in car insurance for new drivers.

Tickets and violations

Speeding tickets, distracted driving citations, and more serious violations such as driving under the influence can raise rates significantly. Insurers typically check motor vehicle records at renewal, so a ticket from months ago may only now be showing up in your price.

A new driver or vehicle on the policy

Adding a teen or newly licensed driver often has a noticeable effect, because inexperienced drivers are statistically more likely to have accidents. Replacing your car with a newer or more expensive model can also raise costs, since repairs and replacement cost more.

Moving to a new address

Even a move within the same city can change your rate. Insurers price by location based on factors such as traffic density, theft, weather, and local repair costs.

Discounts that expired

Some discounts last only for a limited time, such as a new-customer discount or a good-student discount that ends when a driver graduates or turns a certain age. Losing a bundling discount because you moved your home policy elsewhere can also raise your auto premium.

Changes in your credit-based insurance score

In states where the law allows it, insurers may use a credit-based insurance score as one factor in pricing. A change in your credit history could affect your premium at renewal. Several states restrict or prohibit this practice.

Reasons that have nothing to do with you

Higher repair and replacement costs

Modern cars contain sensors, cameras, and electronics that can make even minor repairs expensive. When the cost of parts, labor, and replacement vehicles rises, insurers may raise rates to keep up with what they expect to pay in claims.

Medical and legal costs

Liability and injury coverages pay for medical treatment and, sometimes, legal settlements. When those costs rise in a state or region, premiums for those coverages may follow.

More claims in your area

If your area has experienced more accidents, thefts, or severe weather such as hailstorms and floods, insurers may adjust rates for everyone in that location, regardless of individual driving records.

Company-wide rate changes

Insurers periodically file rate changes with state regulators. In most states, those changes must be filed with or approved by the state's department of insurance. An approved increase can affect all customers with a particular company in a state.

What you can do about it

  1. Ask your insurer why. You can request an explanation of the main factors behind the change. In some situations, insurers must tell you if certain information, such as your credit report, affected your rate.
  2. Check for errors. Review your driving record, claims history, listed drivers, vehicles, and annual mileage. Mistakes happen, and correcting one may lower your premium.
  3. Review your coverage. Make sure your coverage still matches your needs. For an older car that is paid off, it may be worth reviewing whether collision and comprehensive still make sense. Read What Is Full Coverage Car Insurance? before you decide.
  4. Consider your deductibles. A higher deductible may reduce your premium, but only choose an amount you could afford to pay after a claim.
  5. Ask about discounts. Ask about discounts you may now qualify for, such as completing a defensive driving course, bundling policies, paying in full, or enrolling in a usage-based program. Eligibility varies by state and provider.
  6. Shop around. Each insurer prices risk differently, so the same driver can receive very different quotes. Compare identical coverage from several providers. Our guide on comparing quotes explains how.

Be careful about cutting too much

When a premium rises, it is tempting to reduce coverage to the state minimum. Lower liability limits may save money now but could leave you personally responsible for costs after a serious accident. Before reducing coverage, consider what you could lose and read How Much Car Insurance Do You Really Need?

If you think the increase is unfair

If you believe your insurer made an error or treated you unfairly, start by contacting the company in writing. If that does not resolve the issue, you can contact your state's department of insurance, which oversees insurers and handles consumer complaints. Many state departments also publish information about rate filings.

The bottom line

A higher renewal premium may reflect changes in your own situation, your area, or the wider market. Ask your insurer for an explanation, check for errors, review your coverage and discounts, and compare quotes from other providers. Taking these steps at each renewal can help you make sure you are paying for coverage that fits your needs.
