How Much Car Insurance Do You Really Need?

Buying only what the law requires can keep your premium low, but it can also leave you personally responsible for costs that far exceed your policy. Here is a practical way to think through each part of your coverage.

Start with what your state requires

Nearly every state sets a minimum amount of liability insurance that drivers must carry. Some states also require personal injury protection, uninsured motorist coverage, or both. These minimums are the legal floor, not a recommendation. In many states, the required property damage limit may not be enough to replace a newer vehicle, and the bodily injury limits may cover only a fraction of the cost of a serious injury.

Your state's department of insurance or motor vehicle agency publishes the current requirements. Check them first, because rules change. Once you know the floor, you can decide how far above it you want to go.

Liability: protect what you could lose

Liability coverage pays others when you cause an accident. If the costs go beyond your limit, the injured party may try to recover the difference from you directly. That is why many people choose liability limits based on what they have to protect, such as savings, home equity, and future income.

A simple way to think about it:

  • If you have few assets, you may still want more than the minimum. Courts can pursue future wages in some situations, and higher limits are often less expensive than people expect.
  • If you own a home or have significant savings, consider higher limits, such as 100/300/100 or more. Ask a licensed agent how different limits would change your premium.
  • If your assets are substantial, you might look at an umbrella policy, which adds extra liability protection above your auto and home policies. Providers usually require certain underlying limits before they will sell one.

There is no single correct number. The goal is to choose limits you would be comfortable relying on after a serious accident, not just a fender bender.

Collision and comprehensive: what is your car worth to you?

Collision and comprehensive cover damage to your own vehicle. If you have a loan or lease, the lender will almost certainly require both. If you own the car outright, the decision is yours.

Ask yourself two questions. First, what is the car worth today? You can check its approximate value with a used-car pricing guide. Second, could you afford to repair or replace it from your own savings if it were totaled tomorrow? If the answer is no, these coverages are likely worth keeping.

Some drivers use a rough rule of thumb: when the yearly cost of collision and comprehensive approaches a meaningful share of the car's value, it may be time to consider dropping them. That is a personal judgment, though. A low-value car may still be essential if you depend on it and could not easily replace it.

Choosing a deductible

Your deductible is what you pay toward a collision or comprehensive claim before coverage applies. A higher deductible usually lowers your premium, while a lower deductible raises it. The right choice depends on how much cash you could pay quickly without hardship.

If you keep a healthy emergency fund, a higher deductible may make sense. If an unexpected bill of that size would be a serious problem, a lower deductible may be worth the higher premium. Some drivers also choose different deductibles for collision and comprehensive, which many providers allow.

Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage

Not every driver on the road carries insurance, and many carry only the minimum. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage helps protect you and your passengers if the at-fault driver cannot pay. Because your own health insurance may not cover every cost, such as lost wages or high deductibles, many people choose limits that match their own liability coverage. Some states require this coverage, and some let you reject it only in writing.

Medical coverage: MedPay and PIP

Medical payments coverage and personal injury protection help pay medical bills after an accident, no matter who caused it. In no-fault states, PIP is generally mandatory. In other states, MedPay may be optional. If you already have strong health insurance, you might choose a modest amount. If your health plan has a high deductible, or you often carry passengers, extra medical coverage may be worth a closer look.

Optional extras worth a second look

Rental reimbursement, roadside assistance, and gap coverage can be useful depending on your circumstances:

  • Rental reimbursement matters most if you have no other way to get around while your car is in the shop.
  • Roadside assistance may duplicate benefits you already have through an auto club, a credit card, or your vehicle manufacturer.
  • Gap coverage is mainly useful when you owe more on a loan or lease than the car is worth, which is common in the first few years of financing.

A quick checklist

  1. Look up your state's minimum requirements.
  2. List the assets you would want to protect in a lawsuit.
  3. Pick liability limits that reflect those assets, not just the legal minimum.
  4. Decide whether you need collision and comprehensive based on your car's value and your savings.
  5. Choose deductibles you could pay on short notice.
  6. Consider uninsured motorist and medical coverage to protect yourself and your passengers.
  7. Request quotes from several providers, using the same choices.

The bottom line

The amount of car insurance you need depends on your finances, your vehicle, and your state's rules. Treat the legal minimum as a starting point and build up from there based on what you could afford to lose. Prices vary by state and provider, so compare identical coverage from several companies and ask a licensed agent about anything you are unsure of.
