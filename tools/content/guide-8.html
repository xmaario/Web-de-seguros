What Does Homeowners Insurance Cover (and Not Cover)?

A homeowners policy bundles several types of protection into one contract. Knowing what is included, and just as important, what is left out, can help you avoid surprises and fill gaps before you need to file a claim.

The six main parts of a homeowners policy

Most standard homeowners policies in the US are organized into lettered coverage sections. The exact names and limits vary by provider, but the structure is usually similar.

Coverage A: Dwelling

Covers the structure of your home, including the roof, walls, floors, and built-in systems such as plumbing, electrical wiring, and heating. Your dwelling limit should reflect the cost to rebuild your home, not its market value.

Coverage B: Other structures

Covers structures on your property that are not attached to the house, such as a detached garage, shed, or fence. It is commonly set as a percentage of the dwelling limit.

Coverage C: Personal property

Covers your belongings, such as furniture, clothing, electronics, and appliances, whether they are at home or, in many cases, temporarily elsewhere. Certain categories, such as jewelry, firearms, and collectibles, may have lower limits for theft unless you add extra coverage.

Coverage D: Loss of use

Helps pay additional living expenses, such as a hotel, meals, and laundry, if a covered loss makes your home unlivable during repairs.

Coverage E: Personal liability

Helps protect you if you are legally responsible for injuring someone or damaging their property. It may cover legal defense costs and settlements up to your limit. It can apply to incidents at home and, in many cases, away from home.

Coverage F: Medical payments to others

Helps pay small medical bills for guests injured on your property, generally regardless of fault. Limits are usually modest.

Which events are covered?

Policies list the events, called perils, they protect against. There are two main approaches:

  • Named perils coverage protects only against the specific events listed in the policy, such as fire, lightning, windstorm, hail, explosion, theft, vandalism, and certain types of water damage.
  • Open perils coverage, sometimes called "all risk," protects against any cause of loss that is not specifically excluded.

A common setup is open perils coverage for the dwelling and named perils coverage for personal property. Read the "perils insured against" section of your policy to see which approach applies to each part.

Common policy forms

You may see policies described by standardized form names. Insurers may modify these forms, so the details vary, but the general categories are:

  • HO-3: the most common homeowners form. It typically offers open perils coverage on the dwelling and named perils coverage on belongings.
  • HO-5: broader coverage, often open perils for both the dwelling and personal property.
  • HO-6: designed for condo owners, covering the interior of the unit and personal property, while the association's master policy covers the building.
  • HO-4: renters insurance, which covers belongings and liability but not the building. See our renters insurance guide.

What standard policies usually do not cover

These exclusions are common, although the exact wording varies by state and provider:

  • Flood: damage from rising water, storm surge, or overflowing rivers is generally excluded. Flood coverage is usually purchased separately. Read Do You Need Flood Insurance?
  • Earthquake and earth movement: typically excluded but may be available as an endorsement or separate policy.
  • Maintenance problems: wear and tear, rot, mold from long-term leaks, and deterioration are generally not covered.
  • Pests: damage from termites, rodents, and insects is usually excluded.
  • Sewer and drain backup: often excluded unless you add an endorsement.
  • Business activities: running a business from home may require additional coverage.
  • Intentional damage and certain other exclusions defined in the policy.

Water damage: covered or not?

Water damage causes confusion because the answer depends on the source. Sudden and accidental water damage, such as a pipe that bursts, is often covered. Gradual leaks that went unrepaired, flooding from outside, and sewer backups are commonly excluded or limited. If water damage concerns you, ask your provider exactly which situations are covered and whether endorsements are available.

Ways to fill coverage gaps

  1. Endorsements: add-ons such as water backup, scheduled personal property for valuables, or extended replacement cost.
  2. Separate policies: flood or earthquake insurance where you face those risks.
  3. Umbrella insurance: additional liability coverage above your home and auto limits.
  4. Higher limits: review your dwelling and personal property limits after renovations or major purchases.

How to check your own policy

Start with the declarations page, which summarizes your coverages, limits, and deductibles. Then read the sections on perils insured against, exclusions, and conditions. Pay attention to any separate deductibles for wind, hail, or hurricanes, which are explained in Home Insurance Deductibles Explained. If anything is unclear, ask your insurer or a licensed agent to explain it in writing.

The bottom line

A standard homeowners policy can protect your home, belongings, and finances against many common risks, but it does not cover everything. Flood, earthquake, maintenance issues, and some types of water damage are frequent gaps. Review your policy once a year, ask questions, and consider endorsements or separate policies for the risks that matter most where you live.
