How Car Insurance Deductibles Work

Your deductible is the part of a covered claim you pay before your insurer pays the rest. It affects both your premium and what you pay after an accident, so it is worth choosing deliberately.

The basic idea

If you have a $1,000 collision deductible and a covered accident causes $4,000 in damage to your car, the insurer would typically pay $3,000 and you would cover the first $1,000. If the damage is $800, less than your deductible, you would usually pay the whole cost yourself and there would be no claim payment. These numbers are examples only.

Which coverages have a deductible

  • Collision: damage to your car from hitting another vehicle or object. Almost always has a deductible.
  • Comprehensive: theft, vandalism, fire, hail, flooding, falling objects, and animal strikes. Usually has its own deductible, which can differ from collision.
  • Liability: does not have a deductible. It pays others for damage and injuries you cause, up to your limits.
  • Personal injury protection: may have a deductible in some states.
  • Uninsured motorist property damage: may have a small deductible, depending on your state and policy.

When you pay the deductible

You do not usually send the deductible to the insurer. Instead, the insurer subtracts it from the claim payment. If your car is repaired at a shop, you typically pay your deductible to the shop and the insurer pays the remaining covered amount. If your car is totaled, the deductible is subtracted from the car's actual cash value.

Auto deductibles generally apply per claim, not per year. Two separate accidents in a year may mean paying two deductibles.

When another driver is at fault

If another driver caused the accident, you may be able to file a claim with their insurer, which generally does not involve your deductible. If you use your own collision coverage instead, perhaps because it is faster, you may pay your deductible first. Your insurer may then try to recover its costs, and possibly your deductible, from the at-fault driver's insurer through a process called subrogation. This can take time, and full recovery is not guaranteed.

Glass and windshield claims

Windshield damage usually falls under comprehensive coverage. Some insurers offer full glass coverage with no deductible for glass repair or replacement, and a few states have rules that affect windshield deductibles. Many insurers also waive the deductible for a small chip repair, because repairing it early can prevent a costly replacement. Check your policy.

How your deductible affects your premium

A higher deductible generally means a lower premium, because you take on more of each claim. A lower deductible means a higher premium. The size of the difference varies by insurer, vehicle, and location, so ask for quotes at several deductible levels.

A useful way to compare: divide the extra amount you would pay out of pocket with the higher deductible by the yearly premium savings. The result is roughly how many claim-free years it takes to come out ahead. If it would take many years, the higher deductible may not be worth it.

How to choose your deductible

  1. Start with your savings. Pick an amount you could pay right away without borrowing or missing bills.
  2. Consider your car's value. On an older car, a high deductible plus depreciation may leave little to collect after a loss.
  3. Check your lender's rules. If your car is financed or leased, the lender may set a maximum deductible.
  4. Think about local risks. In areas with frequent hail, theft, or animal collisions, a lower comprehensive deductible may be worth more.
  5. Compare real quotes. Look at the actual premium difference, not just the general rule.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a deductible you cannot afford to save a little on your premium.
  • Forgetting that comprehensive and collision can differ. You can often set each one separately.
  • Assuming the deductible applies to liability claims. It does not.
  • Not revisiting it. As your savings grow or your car ages, your ideal deductible may change.

Common questions

Can I change my deductible in the middle of a policy term?

Many insurers allow it, and the premium is adjusted from that date. A change usually applies only to losses that happen after it takes effect, not to a claim already in progress.

Do I pay the deductible if my car is totaled?

Usually yes. The deductible is subtracted from the car's actual cash value. If you have a loan, the remaining payment typically goes to the lender first.

Is a zero deductible a good idea?

A very low or zero deductible means a noticeably higher premium. It can make sense for specific coverages, such as glass, but for collision it often costs more over time than it saves.

The bottom line

Your deductible is a trade-off between what you pay every year and what you pay after a claim.
